\section{Part A: RTL Design and Verification}

\subsection{Full adder}

\texttt{rtl/full\_adder.v} defines module \texttt{full\_adder} with inputs \texttt{a}, \texttt{b},
\texttt{cin} and outputs \texttt{sum}, \texttt{cout}, written only with \texttt{assign} statements:
\begin{align*}
\texttt{sum}  &= a \oplus b \oplus cin\\
\texttt{cout} &= a\,b + b\,cin + a\,cin
\end{align*}
The sum is 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1 (XOR); the carry is the \emph{majority} function, 1 when two or
more inputs are 1.

\subsection{How the array multiplier works}

Binary multiplication follows the same long-multiplication method used on paper, except that every digit
is 0 or 1. Bit $b_i$ of the multiplier $B$ decides what appears in row $i$: the partial product $pp_i$ is a
copy of $A$ when $b_i = 1$ and all zeros when $b_i = 0$, and it is worth $2^i$, i.e.\ shifted $i$ places to the
left. Selecting one bit is a single AND, $pp_i[j] = a[j] \cdot b[i]$, so the four 4-bit partial products need
$4 \times 4 = 16$ AND gates. The product is the sum of the four shifted partial products.

Instead of one large adder, the array accumulates the partial products one row at a time
(Table~\ref{tab:conn}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Row 0} contains only AND gates. Its least significant bit $pp_0[0]$ has nothing to be added
        to, so it is already the product bit $p[0]$; its upper three bits move down to row 1.
  \item \textbf{Rows 1, 2 and 3} are each a 4-bit \emph{ripple-carry adder}: a chain of 4 full adders in
        which every carry-out feeds the carry-in of the next adder, and the first carry-in is 0. Row $i$ adds
        $pp_i$ to the 4 bits arriving from the row above. Three rows of four give the 12 full adders.
  \item Once a row has added, its lowest sum bit can no longer change, so it is a finished product bit
        ($p[1]$, $p[2]$, $p[3]$); the other three sum bits and the final carry move down to the next row.
        Row 3 has no row below it, so its remaining outputs form the upper half of the product, $p[7{:}4]$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table*}[!t]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{clll}
\toprule
\textbf{Row} & \textbf{Inputs to full adder $j$} & \textbf{Outputs} \\
\midrule
0 & no adders (AND gates only) & $p[0] = pp_0[0]$;\; $r_0 = \{0, pp_0[3], pp_0[2], pp_0[1]\}$ \\
1 & $pp_1[j], r_0[j], c_1[j]$ \;($c_1[0]=0$) & $s_1[j], c_1[j{+}1]$;\; $p[1] = s_1[0]$;\; $r_1 = \{c_1[4], s_1[3], s_1[2], s_1[1]\}$ \\
2 & $pp_2[j], r_1[j], c_2[j]$ \;($c_2[0]=0$) & $s_2[j], c_2[j{+}1]$;\; $p[2] = s_2[0]$;\; $r_2 = \{c_2[4], s_2[3], s_2[2], s_2[1]\}$ \\
3 & $pp_3[j], r_2[j], c_3[j]$ \;($c_3[0]=0$) & $s_3[j], c_3[j{+}1]$;\; $p[3] = s_3[0]$;\; $p[7{:}4] = \{c_3[4], s_3[3], s_3[2], s_3[1]\}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\caption{Connections of the array ($j = 0 \ldots 3$; the first carry into each row is $c_i[0] = 0$).}\label{tab:conn}
\end{table*}

\textbf{RTL implementation.} \texttt{rtl/mult\_array.v} has exactly the required ports:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \texttt{clk}: the clock; all registers update on its rising edge.
  \item \texttt{rst\_n}: asynchronous, active-low reset. When it goes to 0, every register is cleared to 0
        immediately, without waiting for a clock edge.
  \item \texttt{a[3:0]}, \texttt{b[3:0]}: the two operands.
  \item \texttt{p[7:0]}: the product, driven directly by a register.
\end{itemize}
Inside the module the data flows through four stages:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item On each rising edge the operands are stored in the 4-bit input registers \texttt{a\_reg} and
        \texttt{b\_reg} (8 flip-flops).
  \item The partial products are formed from the registered values, \texttt{pp\textit{i} = a\_reg \&
        \{4\{b\_reg[\textit{i}]\}\}}, which gives the 16 AND gates.
  \item Twelve \texttt{full\_adder} instances, \texttt{fa10} to \texttt{fa33} (row, position), are connected
        exactly as in Table~\ref{tab:conn}. The carry chains \texttt{c1}, \texttt{c2}, \texttt{c3} have their
        bit 0 tied to 0.
  \item The combinational result \{\texttt{c3[4]}, \texttt{s3[3:0]}, \texttt{s2[0]}, \texttt{s1[0]},
        \texttt{pp0[0]}\} is stored in the output register \texttt{p} (8 flip-flops) on the next rising edge.
\end{enumerate}
This gives 16 flip-flops in total, and the \texttt{*} operator is not used anywhere in the RTL.

\textbf{Latency and throughput.} Because both the inputs and the output are registered, an operand pair
applied before a rising edge is captured at that edge (edge 1), passes through the AND gates and adder rows
during the following clock period, and its product is captured into \texttt{p} at the next edge (edge 2).
The \emph{latency} is therefore 2 clock cycles. The \emph{throughput} is one product per cycle: while one
product is being computed between the two register stages, the input registers can already accept the
next operand pair. The structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:block}.

\subsection{Testbench}

\texttt{tb/tb\_mult.v} (module \texttt{tb\_mult}) is an exhaustive, self-checking testbench: it applies every
input pair, computes the expected product itself, and reports the result without manual inspection.
It runs in this order:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Clock and waveform.} \texttt{always \#5 clk = \~{}clk} gives a 10 ns clock; all signals are
        dumped to \texttt{mult.vcd} from time 0.
  \item \textbf{Reset.} \texttt{rst\_n} = 0 for the first 2 rising edges (5 and 15 ns) and is released at the
        falling edge at 20 ns, so all 16 registers start at 0.
  \item \textbf{All 256 inputs.} Two nested loops set \texttt{a} = 0..15 and \texttt{b} = 0..15. Each new pair is
        applied at a falling edge, half a cycle away from the rising edges, so it is never sampled while changing.
  \item \textbf{Latency and check.} The testbench waits 2 rising edges (edge 1 loads \texttt{a\_reg}/\texttt{b\_reg},
        edge 2 loads \texttt{p}), then 2 ns more, and compares \texttt{p} with \texttt{expected = a * b}. The
        \texttt{*} operator is used only here, as the reference. The comparison uses \texttt{!==}, so an unknown
        (\texttt{x}) output also counts as an error.
  \item \textbf{Reporting.} Every mismatch prints \texttt{Mismatch: a=.. b=.. expected=.. got=..} and increments an
        error counter. After the last vector exactly one summary line is printed,
        \texttt{PASS: 256 vectors checked, 0 errors} or \texttt{FAIL: 256 vectors checked, N errors}.
\end{enumerate}
Each vector takes 2 clock cycles: vector $k$ is applied at $30 + 20k$ ns and checked at $47 + 20k$ ns, so the
run ends at $47 + 20 \times 255 = 5147$ ns, matching \texttt{\$finish called at 5147000 (1ps)}.

\section{Part B: Logic Synthesis with Yosys}

\subsection{Synthesis script}

Synthesis translates the behavioural Verilog into a \emph{netlist}: a list of real SKY130 standard cells
and the wires that connect them. Each cell is a fixed, pre-designed layout whose area and delay are given in
the \emph{Liberty} file (\texttt{.lib}). The key step is \texttt{abc}, which performs \emph{technology mapping}:
it chooses the combination of library gates that implements the logic with good area and delay. Because of
\texttt{-flatten}, ABC sees the whole multiplier as one block instead of 12 separate full adders, so it can
optimise across adder boundaries. Table~\ref{tab:synth} lists the steps of \texttt{synth/synth.tcl}.

\subsection{Gate-level (netlist) simulation}

Synthesis could, in principle, break the design (for example by mapping a register wrongly). To check this,
we ran the \emph{same} testbench on the synthesised netlist instead of the RTL. The simulator is given the
netlist together with the SKY130 cell models (\texttt{primitives.v} and \texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd.v}):
\texttt{-DFUNCTIONAL} uses the logic-only version of each cell, and \texttt{-DUNIT\_DELAY=\#1} adds a tiny delay
to every cell so that signals change in a clean order.

\begin{lstlisting}[frame=none, xleftmargin=1.5em]
CELLS=$PDK_ROOT/sky130A/libs.ref/sky130_fd_sc_hd/verilog
iverilog -g2012 -DFUNCTIONAL -DUNIT_DELAY=#1 -o gls \
    tb/tb_mult.v $CELLS/primitives.v $CELLS/sky130_fd_sc_hd.v \
    synth/mult_array_netlist.v
vvp gls
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Result:} \texttt{PASS: 256 vectors checked, 0 errors}. The netlist gives the same product as the RTL
for every input, so synthesis did not change the behaviour.

\section{Part C: Static Timing Analysis with OpenSTA}

\subsection{STA script and constraints}

Static timing analysis (STA) finds the maximum speed without running any simulation: it traces every path
from one flip-flop to the next and adds up the cell delays taken from the Liberty file. Data launched at one
rising edge must arrive and settle before the next rising edge, which gives the setup condition
\[
t_{clk\rightarrow Q} + t_{logic} + t_{setup} \le T ,
\]
where $t_{clk\rightarrow Q}$ is the delay of the launching flip-flop, $t_{logic}$ the delay through the gates and
$t_{setup}$ the time the data must be stable before the capturing edge. The \emph{slack} of a path is its
required time ($T - t_{setup}$) minus its arrival time: positive slack means the path meets timing, negative
means it fails. The smallest slack in the design is the worst negative slack (WNS), and the path that has it
is the \emph{critical path}. \texttt{sta/sta.tcl} runs the steps below.

worst slack max 7.40                       (report: +7.404 ns)

T_min = T - WNS   = 10.000 - 7.404 = 2.596 ns
F_max = 1000/T_min = 1000 / 2.596   = 385.21 MHz

$ STA_PERIOD=2.60 sta -exit sta/sta.tcl    (T = T_min rounded to 0.01 ns)
worst slack max 0.00                       (report: +0.004 ns)
\end{lstlisting}

At $T = 2.60$ ns the slack is +0.004 ns, effectively zero, which confirms $T_{min}$. The small positive
value comes from rounding 2.596 ns up to 2.60 ns.

\subsection{Comparison with the $2N = 8$ full-adder estimate}

\textbf{Full-adder stages.} The path passes through 6 full adders: FA[1,1] $\rightarrow$ FA[2,1] $\rightarrow$
FA[2,2] $\rightarrow$ FA[2,3] $\rightarrow$ FA[3,2] $\rightarrow$ FA[3,3]. It follows the carry along row 1, down
into row 2, along row 2, and then through the sums and carries of rows 2 and 3 into product bit 6. Seven of the
8 logic cells belong to these adders; only \cell{\_083\_} (\cell{and2\_0}) is a plain AND gate.

\textbf{Delay per stage.} The 8 logic cells add 2.065 ns ($2.460 - 0.395$), about 0.26 ns per cell. Each full
adder costs only about one cell because the path uses either its carry or its sum, never both: a carry is one
\cell{maj3} or AND-OR cell, and a sum is one \cell{xor3}/\cell{xnor3} cell.

\textbf{Comparison with 2N.} Six full-adder stages in 8 cells agree well with the estimate of about $2N = 8$
full-adder delays, which is only an approximation of the longest chain. The match cannot be exact because
\texttt{synth -flatten} removes the \texttt{full\_adder} boundaries and ABC merges AND gates and parts of several
adders into single compound cells.

\section{Answers to the Report Questions}

\subsection*{Q1. The array needs 16 AND gates and 12 full adders. How many cells did synthesis produce for the logic? Explain why the number of cells is not simply 16 + 12.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q1. Logic cell count}

Synthesis produced 73 cells. 16 of them are flip-flops, so the logic uses \textbf{57 cells} (405.39 \umsq),
not 28. ``AND gate'' and ``full adder'' are blocks in our RTL, not single library cells. Because of
\texttt{-flatten}, Yosys removes the \texttt{full\_adder} boundaries and ABC rebuilds the whole logic from
whichever SKY130 cells give a good area and delay (Table~\ref{tab:cells}). The count changes for four reasons:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{One full adder needs at least two cells.} Its sum becomes an XOR/XNOR cell (7 in total) and its
        carry a \cell{maj3} cell or a small NAND/AND-OR network. ABC cannot use SKY130's two-output full-adder
        cell \cell{fa\_1} (the log reports ``Detected 9 multi-output cells''), so 12 full adders alone need
        about 24 cells or more.
  \item \textbf{AND gates are merged into other cells.} Most partial-product ANDs disappear inside compound
        gates such as \cell{a21oi}, \cell{a21boi}, \cell{o2111a} and \cell{nand4}; only 6 plain AND cells remain.
  \item \textbf{Constant inputs make some adders simpler.} The first carry of every row and the top bit of $r_0$
        are always 0, so those full adders shrink to half adders.
  \item \textbf{A few inverting cells are needed} (\cell{clkinv}, \cell{nor2b}) to match signal polarities.
\end{itemize}
The exact number also depends on how the RTL is written and on the tool version, because ABC is a heuristic.
For example, writing the carry as \texttt{(a\&b)|(a\&cin)|(b\&cin)} instead of our
\texttt{(a\&b)|(b\&cin)|(a\&cin)} (the same logic) gives 61 cells with the same tools. In every case the count
is well above 28.

\subsection*{Q2. What is the critical path of your design, from which register to which register? How many cells are on it, and how does this compare with the estimate of about 2N full-adder delays?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q2. Critical path}

The critical path starts at the input register \texttt{b\_reg[0]} (\texttt{\_118\_}) and ends at the output
register \texttt{p[6]} (\texttt{\_112\_}), whose input is the sum of the last full adder, FA[3,3]. It has
\textbf{10 cells}: the launching flip-flop, 8 logic cells (\cell{and2} $\rightarrow$ \cell{or3} $\rightarrow$
\cell{and4} $\rightarrow$ \cell{o2111a} $\rightarrow$ \cell{o21ai} $\rightarrow$ \cell{xnor3} $\rightarrow$
\cell{maj3} $\rightarrow$ \cell{xor3}) and the capturing flip-flop. The data arrives after 2.460 ns, which gives
$T_{min}$ = 2.596 ns.

By matching every net with the RTL signals (Table~\ref{tab:critpath}), the path passes through \textbf{6 full adders}:
FA[1,1] $\rightarrow$ FA[2,1] $\rightarrow$ FA[2,2] $\rightarrow$ FA[2,3] $\rightarrow$ FA[3,2] $\rightarrow$ FA[3,3].
It follows the carry along row 1 and row 2 and then the sums and carries of rows 2 and 3 into $p[6]$. Seven of
the 8 logic cells belong to these adders, at about 0.26 ns per cell. This agrees well with the estimate of about
$2N = 8$ full-adder delays; each adder costs only one cell because the path uses either its carry or its sum,
never both.

\subsection*{Q3. What percentage of the total cell area is flip-flops? Why is this percentage large for such a small design?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q3. Flip-flop area}

Flip-flops take 400.38 of 805.77 \umsq, which is \textbf{49.69 \%} of the cell area, even though they are only
16 of the 73 cells (22 \%). There are two reasons:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{A flip-flop is a big cell.} One \cell{dfrtp\_1} is 25.02 \umsq: 6.7 times a \cell{nand2\_1}
        (3.75 \umsq), about 3.5 times the average logic cell (7.1 \umsq), and larger than any logic cell in the
        design. A flip-flop with asynchronous reset contains two latches plus clock and reset circuitry, a few
        dozen transistors compared with 4 for a NAND2.
  \item \textbf{Scaling.} The array logic grows with $N^2$ (16 ANDs and 12 adders), but the registers grow only
        with $4N$ (16 flip-flops). At $N = 4$ the logic is still small, so the registers dominate. At 8 bits the
        flip-flop share drops to about 32 \% (Q6).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Q4. Why is the post-route F\textsubscript{max} lower than the F\textsubscript{max} from Part C? Which kinds of cells did the flow add, and why is each kind needed?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q4. Post-route Fmax and added cells}

The post-route $F_{max}$ is \textbf{150.70 MHz}, against 385.21 MHz from Part C, about 2.6 times lower. The two
numbers are measured under very different conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Corner.} Part C used only the typical library (tt, 1.80 V, 25 $^\circ$C). LibreLane signs off at
        9 corners and reports the worst one, the slow corner (ss, 1.60 V, 100 $^\circ$C), where cells are about
        twice as slow. The same layout reaches 302.9 MHz at the typical corner, so the corner alone explains a
        factor of about 2.
  \item \textbf{Real wires.} Part C had no wire delay. After routing, 2362 \um{} of wire and 552 vias add
        resistance and capacitance: on the critical path the logic delay grows by 0.374 ns and CLK$\rightarrow$Q
        by 0.077 ns.
  \item \textbf{Real clock.} Part C used an ideal clock. The real clock tree has insertion delay and 0.26--0.28 ns
        of skew between flip-flops.
  \item \textbf{Stricter constraints.} LibreLane adds 0.25 ns of clock uncertainty and a 5 \% timing derate to
        every path. (Its input and output delays are also larger, 20 \% of the period = 1.4 ns, but these only
        affect the I/O paths, not our register-to-register critical path.)
  \item \textbf{Different netlist.} LibreLane re-synthesises the RTL with its own script (67 cells), so the
        post-route critical path, \texttt{a\_reg[0]} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{p[6]} with 9 logic cells, is not the
        same as in Part C.
\end{enumerate}
At the same typical corner, items 2--5 add 0.705 ns to the minimum period (Table~\ref{tab:fmaxbreak}).

The flow added these cells (Table~\ref{tab:added}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Buffers (22):} 17 \cell{buf\_2} I/O port buffers, one on each input and output pin, which
        separate the core from outside drivers and loads; 2 \cell{clkdlybuf4s50\_1} fanout buffers, which split the
        reset net that drives all 16 flip-flops; and 3 \cell{clkbuf\_16} clock-tree buffers, which deliver the
        clock to the 16 flip-flops with low skew.
  \item \textbf{Tap cells (245):} connect the n-well to VPWR and the substrate to VGND at regular spacing to
        prevent latch-up.
  \item \textbf{Decap cells (1314):} small capacitors between VPWR and VGND that reduce supply noise.
  \item \textbf{Fill cells (247):} fill the gaps so that wells, implants and power rails stay continuous along
        every row, which DRC requires.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Q5. What does a DRC check verify, and what does an LVS check verify? Why must both pass before a chip can be manufactured?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q5. DRC and LVS}

\textbf{DRC} (design rule check) checks the \emph{shapes} in the layout against the foundry's rules for every
layer: minimum width and spacing, enclosure and overlap, via rules, metal density and antenna ratios. For
example, two metal wires placed closer than the minimum spacing might short together when printed. The rules
describe what the factory can make reliably, so a violation can cause shorts, open wires or very low yield.

\textbf{LVS} (layout versus schematic) checks the \emph{circuit}. It extracts the transistors and their
connections from the layout and compares them with the netlist: the same devices, the same nets and the same
pins. For example, a missing via or two nets accidentally touching would make the layout a different circuit
from the one we designed.

Each check catches what the other cannot: a layout can follow every drawing rule but be the wrong circuit, or be
the right circuit but impossible to manufacture. Masks are very expensive and a chip cannot be fixed after it is
made, so both must pass first. Our final run has \textbf{0 DRC errors} in both Magic and KLayout (the router's
intermediate violations, 28 $\rightarrow$ 3 $\rightarrow$ 3 $\rightarrow$ 0, were all fixed), \textbf{0 LVS errors}
in Netgen (``Circuits match uniquely'') and 0 antenna violations.

\subsection*{Q6. Using the formulas in Section 2, estimate how the number of full adders and the critical path delay would change if the multiplier were 8 bits wide instead of 4. Show your working.}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Q6. Scaling to 8 bits}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{N = 4 (ours)} & \textbf{N = 8 (estimate)} & \textbf{Change} \\
\midrule
AND gates & $N^2$ & 16 & 64 & $\times 4$ \\
Full adders & $N(N-1)$ & 12 & 56 & $\times 4.67$ \\
Flip-flops & $4N$ & 16 & 32 & $\times 2$ \\
Critical path & $\approx 2N$ full-adder delays & 8 & 16 & $\times 2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\caption{Scaling from 4 to 8 bits.}
\end{table}

\textbf{Working.} From Part C, the logic on the critical path takes 2.065 ns, and the flip-flop overhead is
0.395 ns (CLK$\rightarrow$Q) + 0.135 ns (setup) = 0.530 ns. The logic delay grows with the path length ($2N$),
so it doubles from 4 to 8 bits, while the flip-flop overhead stays the same:
\begin{align*}
T_{min}(8\text{-bit}) &\approx 0.530 + 2 \times 2.065 = 4.66 \text{ ns},\\
F_{max}(8\text{-bit}) &\approx 1000 / 4.66 \approx 215 \text{ MHz}.
\end{align*}
As a cross-check with our measured path: for $N = 4$ it crosses 6 full adders ($2N - 2$), about 0.344 ns each.
The same pattern gives 14 adders for $N = 8$, so $T_{min} \approx 0.530 + 14 \times 0.344 = 5.35$ ns
(187 MHz). The 8-bit multiplier would therefore run at roughly \textbf{185--215 MHz} instead of 385 MHz
(typical corner, before layout).

So the number of full adders grows about 4.7 times (with $N^2$), but the critical path grows only about 2 times
(with $N$), and $T_{min}$ rises less than 2 times (2.60 $\rightarrow$ 4.7--5.4 ns) because the flip-flop overhead
does not change. For area, scaling the logic by the number of blocks, $(64 + 56)/(16 + 12) \approx 4.3$, gives
about 1740 \umsq{} of logic; with 32 flip-flops (801 \umsq) the total is about 2540 \umsq{}, and the flip-flop share
falls to about 32 \%.
